Code Review Comments in English: Clear, Polite and Direct

Published 7 November 2026 • Updated 14 November 2026 • By Linley Bignoux

english-for-developerscode-reviewswritingtonephrases

The situation

When I worked as a developer, I often worked inside a build someone else had started. In one, I found five real faults in the layer underneath the parts everyone looked at. Finding them was the technical part. Writing five comments that the original developer would accept, and not argue with, was the communication part.

Written comments have no tone of voice, no smile and no "I'm on your side". A short comment that sounds normal in your head can read as harsh on screen. The same is true when you review a colleague's report, presentation or design in any job.

Why it matters

  • Comments stay in writing. The whole team can read them, often for years.
  • A harsh comment starts an argument, and the fix takes longer.
  • A vague comment gets ignored, or the author fixes the wrong thing.

How to do it: three parts

  1. What you see: "This loop runs a query for every order."
  2. Why it matters: "With 1,000 orders, that's 1,000 queries."
  3. A suggestion, as a question: "Could we fetch them all in one query?"

Many teams also start comments with a label, so the author knows how important each one is:

LabelMeaningExample
nit:Small and optional"nit: would orderTotal be clearer than ot?"
question:You want to understand"question: why is this check here and not in the service?"
suggestion:An idea the author can accept or not"suggestion: we could reuse the date helper here."
blocking:Must be fixed before merging"blocking: this exposes the API key in the logs."

Better phrases

Instead ofTry
"This is wrong.""I think this will fail when the list is empty. Could we add a check?"
"Why did you do this?""Can you help me understand why this is here instead of in the service?"
"Bad performance.""This runs a query inside the loop. Could we fetch everything in one query?"
"You forgot the tests.""Could we add a test for the empty case?"
"Change the name.""nit: what about a more descriptive name, like orderTotal?"

The same approach works outside code. Reviewing a colleague's report: "In section 2, the totals don't match the table. Could you check the March figures?"

Replying to review comments

  • "Good catch, fixed."
  • "I see your point. I kept it this way because… Happy to change it if you still prefer the other way."
  • "Good idea. Can we do that in a separate pull request, so this one stays small?"

A full example: weak and strong

Weak:

"Wrong logic. Fix."

The author doesn't know what's wrong, why it matters, or what you want, and it sounds angry.

Strong:

"blocking: I think this condition skips customers without a postcode, so their orders won't be shipped. Could we handle the missing case? Happy to pair on it if that helps."

What you see, why it matters, a suggestion as a question, the label, and an offer to help.

Common mistakes

MistakeInstead ofSay
Orders"You should change this.""Could we change this?" / "What about…?"
Missing "it""I think is better to use a map.""I think it's better to use a map."
Question word order"Why you did this?""Why did you do this?"
"Please to""Please to check the test.""Please check the test."
Absolute words"This never works.""This fails when the date is empty."

Try it yourself

Find a review comment you wrote recently. Rewrite it in three parts: what you see, why it matters, and a suggestion as a question. Add a label if your team uses them.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I disagree with a review comment politely?

Thank the reviewer, then give your reason and offer a choice: Thanks, I see your point. I kept it this way because the API returns the list unsorted. Happy to change it if you still prefer the other way.

What does nit mean in a code review?

Nit, short for nitpick, labels a small, optional point such as a name or formatting. It tells the author the comment is not blocking. Check what labels your team uses.

Is you should rude in a code review?

Not always, but in writing it can sound like an order. Could we, What about or I'd suggest sound more collaborative and keep the same meaning.
