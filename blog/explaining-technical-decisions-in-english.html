Explaining a Technical Decision to Non-Technical People in English

Published 13 November 2026 • By Linley Bignoux

english-for-developersbusiness-englishmeetingsplain-englishphrases

The situation

When I worked as a developer, some decisions were expensive to change later: where a piece of the system lived, who owned each part, what would happen when something failed. The people who had to approve those decisions were often managers and clients, not developers.

Engineers explaining a design choice, IT teams proposing a new system, and logistics managers explaining a change to the warehouse software all face the same task: explain a technical decision so that someone without your background can make a good choice.

Why it matters

  • Good ideas get rejected when nobody understands them.
  • Jargon makes listeners feel excluded, and people rarely approve what they don't understand.
  • Clear explanations build trust, and the next decision is easier to get approved.

How to do it: five steps

  1. The decision: "We need to decide where to store customer files."
  2. The options: two or three, no more.
  3. The trade-off: what each option costs in money, time or risk.
  4. Your recommendation, with one main reason.
  5. What it means for them: the cost, the date, and what they need to do.

Jargon into plain English

Instead ofTry
"We need to refactor the module.""We need to reorganise part of the code so future changes are faster."
"There's a lot of technical debt.""Some earlier shortcuts are now slowing us down."
"It doesn't scale.""It works now, but it will struggle when we have more customers."
"We're deprecating the old API.""We'll stop supporting the old connection, so partners need to switch by March."
"The tolerance is too tight.""The part needs to be made more precisely than our supplier can manage."

Useful phrases

Presenting options

  • "We looked at three options."
  • "The main difference is…"

Explaining trade-offs

  • "It's cheaper, but it's harder to change later."
  • "The downside is… / The risk is…"

Recommending

  • "I'd recommend option B, because…"
  • "On balance, I think… is the safer choice."

Checking understanding

  • "Does that make sense so far?"
  • "Would it help if I showed you an example?"

A full example: weak and strong

Weak:

"So basically we should use a managed service with autoscaling instead of self-hosting because of the latency and the technical debt, it's easy, everybody does it now."

Strong:

"We need to decide where the new booking system runs. We can run it on our own server, which is cheaper now, or use a hosted service, which costs about 20% more but handles busy periods for us. I'd recommend the hosted service, because last December our own server slowed down for two days. The main downside is the monthly cost."

Decision, two options, the trade-off in money and risk, a recommendation with a real reason, and the downside, with no jargon.

Common mistakes

MistakeInstead ofSay
Making it sound simple"It's easy.""It's a well-tested approach."
Testing the listener"Do you understand?""Does that make sense so far?"
Sounding impatient"Like I said…""To come back to the main point…"
Too many optionsSix options, all in detailTwo or three, and your recommendation
Hiding the downsideOnly the benefits"The main downside is…"

Try it yourself

Choose a decision from your work. Explain it in five sentences to someone outside your team: the decision, the options, the trade-off, your recommendation and what it means for them. Remove every word they might not know.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I avoid jargon when I explain technical work?

Describe the effect instead of the technology. Say how long the customer waits rather than latency, and some earlier shortcuts are slowing us down rather than technical debt. If a technical word is necessary, explain it once in plain English.

How do I check someone understood without being rude?

Ask Does that make sense so far? or Would an example help? rather than Do you understand?, which can sound like a test.

How do I recommend an option clearly but politely?

Name the option and give one main reason: I'd recommend option B, because it's easier to change later. Then mention the main downside, so the listener trusts your recommendation.
