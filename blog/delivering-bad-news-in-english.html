Delivering Bad News in English: Delays, Problems and Missed Deadlines

Published 13 November 2026 • By Linley Bignoux

business-englishemailsclientstonephrases

The situation

When I worked as a developer, one of the emails I built told customers that their payment had failed. It had to be clear enough that people acted on it, calm enough that it didn't sound like an accusation, and polite without hiding the message.

That's the same challenge as telling a client that a shipment will be late, telling your manager the project will miss its deadline, or telling a supplier their invoice is wrong. In a second language it's even harder, because the line between polite and unclear, or between clear and rude, is easy to miss.

Why it matters

  • Too direct sounds rude. "The project is late" can sound cold, even when you didn't mean it that way.
  • Too soft means the message is missed. If the bad news is hidden in the last line, people don't act on it.
  • How you say it affects trust. Clients and managers remember how you handled a problem more than the problem itself.

How to do it: five steps

  1. Prepare the listener with a short signal: "I'm afraid I have some bad news about the Friday delivery."
  2. Say the news clearly, early, in one sentence.
  3. Give the reason briefly. One sentence is usually enough.
  4. Offer a solution or options.
  5. Take ownership of the next step, with a time.

Softer, but still clear

Softening changes the tone, not the message. The listener should still know exactly what happened.

DirectSofter, still clear
"The project is late.""I'm afraid the project is running about a week behind schedule."
"We can't deliver on Friday.""Unfortunately, we won't be able to deliver on Friday."
"Your payment failed.""We weren't able to process your payment."
"That's not possible.""That would be difficult. What we can do is…"
"You made a mistake on the invoice.""There seems to be an error on the invoice."

Useful phrases

Signalling bad news

  • "I'm afraid I have some bad news about…"
  • "I wanted to let you know as soon as possible that…"

Giving options

  • "We have two options. We can…, or we can…"
  • "What we can do is…"

Taking ownership

  • "That was our mistake, and I'm sorry for the inconvenience."
  • "I'll personally make sure that…"

A full example: an email about a delay

Weak:

Subject: Update
Hi Anna, sorry sorry, we had many problems this week with the supplier and also some people were sick, so we will try to send soon, maybe next week. Sorry again.

The subject says nothing, the apology is repeated, the reason is long, and "try", "soon" and "maybe" mean Anna can't plan anything.

Strong:

Subject: Order 4471: new delivery date

Hi Anna,

I'm afraid your order won't arrive on Friday as planned. Our supplier delivered the materials three days late.

We can deliver the full order next Wednesday, or send the first half this Friday and the rest on Wednesday. Please let me know which works better for you.

I'm sorry for the inconvenience, and I'll confirm the delivery time as soon as you choose.

Best regards,
Daniel

The news is in the first line, the reason is one sentence, there are two real options with dates, and the apology appears once.

Common mistakes

MistakeInstead ofSay
Over-apologising"Sorry, so sorry, sorry again."Apologise once: "I'm sorry for the delay."
Hiding the newsThree paragraphs, then the delay at the endThe news in the first one or two sentences
Vague timing"Soon.""By Wednesday at 5 pm."
Sounding unsure"We will try to send it.""We will send it on Wednesday."
Blaming the client"You sent the wrong file.""It looks like we received an older version of the file."

A note on culture: people from different countries expect different levels of directness. In international English, a message that is softened but still clear is usually the safest choice.

Try it yourself

Write a five-sentence email about a real delay or problem from your work: the signal, the news, the reason, the options and the next step. Then check: is the news in the first two sentences? Is there a real date?

Frequently Asked Questions

What does I'm afraid mean in business English?

It means unfortunately. I'm afraid the delivery will be late is a polite way to give bad news. It has nothing to do with being scared.

How do I apologise professionally in English?

Apologise once, clearly, then move to the solution: I'm sorry for the delay. Here's what we're doing to fix it. Apologising again and again makes the message weaker, not stronger.

Should I give bad news by email or in a call?

For serious news, call first and then confirm by email, so the details are in writing. For small delays, a clear email is usually enough.
