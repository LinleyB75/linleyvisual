Giving a Project Update in a Meeting: What Worked, What Didn't, and What's Next

Published 6 October 2026 • Updated 14 November 2026 • By Linley Bignoux

business-englishmeetingsproject-managementgrammarphrases

The situation

When I worked as a developer, I built a dark promotional email, and afterwards I wrote up what broke, what fixed it and what to watch for next time. That's a project review, and it's the same thing you do every week in a project meeting or a retrospective: say where things are, what went well, what didn't, and what happens next.

In 18 years of stock control I gave the same kind of update about stock counts, system changes and supplier problems. The topic changes. The structure doesn't.

Why it matters

  • Managers make decisions from your update, about budget, deadlines and people.
  • Hidden problems get bigger. A risk mentioned early is cheap; a surprise at the deadline is expensive.
  • A clear update makes you look in control, even when the project isn't going perfectly.

How to do it: five parts

  1. Status in one line: "We're on track for the March launch."
  2. Progress since last time: "We've finished testing at two sites."
  3. Problems, and what you did: "The scanner software failed at the second site. We fixed it with an update."
  4. Risks: "The biggest risk is the supplier delivery date."
  5. Next steps and what you need: "Next week we start training. I need a decision on overtime by Friday."

Status in one line

StatusPhrase
Green: on track"We're on track for the March launch."
Amber: at risk"We're at risk on the delivery date because the supplier is late."
Red: off track"We're off track by about two weeks. Here's the plan to recover."

Useful phrases

What went well

  • "The main win this week was…"
  • "… went better than expected."

What didn't go to plan

  • "One thing that didn't go to plan was…"
  • "We underestimated…"

Lessons and risks

  • "Next time, we'll…"
  • "The biggest risk is… If that happens, we'll…"

Asking for something

  • "I need a decision on… by Friday."
  • "It would help if…"

The right tenses

UseTenseExample
Progress, no time givenPresent perfect"We've finished testing."
Finished, with a timePast simple"We finished testing on Tuesday."
Plans already decidedgoing to / present continuous"We're starting training on Monday."
Decisions and promiseswill"I'll send the new plan tomorrow."

A full example: weak and strong

Weak:

"So, we did many things, the testing is finished I think, there were some problems with the scanners but now is OK, and the supplier maybe is late, we will see."

Strong:

"We're on track for the March launch, with one risk. We've finished testing at both sites. The scanner software failed at the second site last week, and we fixed it with an update. The main risk is the supplier: if they deliver after the 10th, training moves by a week. I need a decision on overtime by Friday."

Common mistakes

MistakeInstead ofSay
Present perfect with a past time"We have finished it yesterday.""We finished it yesterday."
Word order"The project is late a little.""The project is slightly behind."
Sounding passive"We will see.""We'll decide by Friday."
Missing subject"Now is OK.""It's working now."
Hiding the riskRisk mentioned last, quicklyStatus first, including the risk

Frequently Asked Questions

What does on track mean in a project update?

It means the project is going to finish on time and as planned. At risk means it might not, and off track means it won't without a change.

What is a RAG status?

Red, amber and green: a quick traffic-light way to show status. Green is on track, amber is at risk and red is off track. Always add one sentence explaining the colour.

Should I use the present perfect or the past simple in an update?

Use the present perfect for progress without a time: We've finished testing. Use the past simple with a time: We finished testing on Tuesday. Never mix them: not We have finished it yesterday.
