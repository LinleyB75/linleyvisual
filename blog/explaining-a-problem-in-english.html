Explaining a Problem at Work in English: What Happened, Why, and What's Next

Published 13 November 2026 • By Linley Bignoux

business-englishmeetingsproblemsspeakingphrases

The situation

When I worked as a developer, one automatic email could fail in seven different places between the system that sent it and the customer's inbox. When something went wrong, the first question was always "What happened?", and the people asking were usually not technical.

Before that, I spent 18 years in stock control and inventory, and the question was the same: a delivery didn't arrive, the stock figures didn't match, an order was missing. Every job has problems. The problem can be complicated, but the explanation can't be.

Why it matters

  • People make decisions from your explanation. If it's unclear, they decide badly or they decide late.
  • Unclear explanations create panic or blame. When people don't understand what happened, they fill the gap with the worst version.
  • A clear explanation shows you're in control, even when the news is bad.

How to do it: five parts

  1. What happened: the facts, with no blame.
  2. The impact: who or what was affected, and how much.
  3. The cause: or, honestly, that you don't know yet.
  4. What you've done: the fix, or what you're doing now.
  5. What happens next: the next step, and when.

The same five parts work in any job. Here they are for a tech problem and a logistics problem:

PartTech exampleLogistics example
What happened"Some customers didn't receive their receipt emails yesterday.""Yesterday's delivery to the city store didn't arrive."
Impact"About 200 customers were affected.""The store was out of three products for one day."
Cause"The data sent to the email system was missing a field.""The order was entered with the wrong delivery date."
What we've done"We've fixed the data and resent the emails.""We sent an urgent delivery this morning."
Next"We're adding a check so it can't happen again.""We're adding a second check on delivery dates."

Useful phrases

Describing what happened

  • "I wanted to update you on an issue with…"
  • "Yesterday afternoon, we noticed that…"

Explaining the cause

  • "It was caused by… / It happened because…"
  • "The root cause was…"

When you don't know yet

  • "We're still looking into the cause. I'll update you by 3 pm."
  • "We haven't confirmed the cause yet, but the impact is limited to…"

The fix and the next step

  • "We've put a temporary fix in place."
  • "To stop it happening again, we're…"

A full example: weak and strong

Weak:

"There was a big problem with the system yesterday, many things were not working, we don't know exactly why, maybe it's the data, but now I think it's OK."

The listener still doesn't know what was affected, how badly, or whether it will happen again. "Big problem", "many things" and "I think it's OK" make it sound worse and less controlled than it was.

Strong:

"Yesterday, about 200 customers didn't receive their receipt emails. The data sent to the email system was missing a field. We've fixed it and resent the emails this morning, and we're adding a check so it can't happen again."

Four sentences: what happened, the impact, the cause, the fix and the next step.

Common mistakes

MistakeInstead ofSay
Blaming a person"Maria put the wrong date.""The order was entered with the wrong date."
Guessing the cause"Maybe it's the server.""We haven't confirmed the cause yet."
Present tense for the past"Yesterday the system don't send the emails.""Yesterday the system didn't send the emails."
Saying it's solved when it isn't"The problem is solved.""We've put a temporary fix in place. The full fix is planned for Friday."
Too much technical detailA five-minute story of the investigationThe five parts, then: "Happy to go into the details if that's useful."

A tip on the passive voice: "The order was entered with the wrong date" focuses on what happened, not who did it. In a problem report that sounds calm and professional, and it keeps the conversation on the fix.

Try it yourself

Think of a problem from your work this month. Explain it in five sentences: what happened, the impact, the cause, what you did, and what happens next. Say it out loud. If it takes more than 30 seconds, cut the story and keep the facts.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I explain a problem without blaming anyone?

Describe the facts, and use the passive voice when the person isn't important: The order was entered with the wrong date, instead of Maria entered the wrong date. Focus on what happened and what you are doing about it.

What should I say if I don't know the cause yet?

Say so clearly and give a time for the next update: We haven't confirmed the cause yet. I'll update you by 3 pm. Guessing and being wrong is worse than saying you don't know.

How much detail should I give a manager or client?

Usually only what they need to make a decision: what happened, the impact, and what happens next. Offer more detail if they want it.
