Asking Clarifying Questions in English Without Sounding Unsure

Published 6 October 2026 • Updated 14 November 2026 • By Linley Bignoux

business-englishspeakingquestionsclientsgrammar

The situation

When I worked as a developer, a client reported that the footer text in an email turned black. It only happened on one kind of device, in one email application, in dark mode. Finding the fix depended on asking the right questions: Which device? Which application? Light or dark mode? Every time, or only sometimes?

Every job has the same moment. A manager says "the report is wrong", a client says "can you make it better?", a colleague says "send me the file". Guessing wastes time. Asking well saves it.

Why it matters

  • Guessing leads to the wrong work, done twice.
  • Good questions sound professional, not weak. They show you want to get it right.
  • In a second language, it's tempting to say "yes" and work it out later. That's usually the most expensive choice.

Four kinds of clarifying question

PurposeExample
Checking meaning"When you say urgent, do you mean today or this week?"
Narrowing down"Does it happen on every device, or only on Mac?"
Confirming"So, just to confirm, you need the March figures by Friday. Is that right?"
Asking to repeat"Sorry, I didn't catch the last part. Could you say that again?"

Vague request, better question

They sayYou ask
"The report is wrong.""Which part looks wrong: the totals or the dates?"
"Can you make it better?""What would you like to change most: the layout, the wording or the length?"
"Send it as soon as possible.""Would Thursday morning work for you?"
"It doesn't work on my computer.""Could you tell me which browser you're using?"

Indirect questions sound more polite

Indirect questions start with a polite opening, and then use normal statement word order, with no inversion:

DirectIndirect
"Where is the file?""Could you tell me where the file is?"
"When does the meeting start?""Do you know when the meeting starts?"
"Which version are you using?""Could you let me know which version you're using?"

A full example: weak and strong

Client: "The footer looks wrong in the email."

Weak:

"OK, I will check." (Then hours of guessing.)

Strong:

"Thanks for letting me know. Could you tell me which device and email application you're using? And does it happen in light mode too, or only in dark mode? A screenshot would really help."

Three short questions, in a sensible order, and one request. The answer usually points straight at the cause.

Common mistakes

MistakeInstead ofSay
Too abrupt"What?""Sorry, could you say that again?"
Missing "it""Can you repeat?""Could you repeat that?"
Indirect question word order"Can you tell me where is the file?""Can you tell me where the file is?"
Double negative"I didn't understand nothing.""I didn't understand that part."
Too many at onceFive questions in one long sentenceTwo or three short questions, in order

Try it yourself

Think of the last vague request you received at work. Write two clarifying questions and one confirming sentence for it. Use at least one indirect question.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I ask someone to repeat without sounding rude?

Say Sorry, could you say that again? or Sorry, I didn't catch the last part. Both are normal and polite in meetings. Avoid What?, which sounds abrupt.

What is the word order in indirect questions?

After an opening like Could you tell me, use normal statement order: Could you tell me where the file is? not Could you tell me where is the file?

How do I confirm that I understood correctly?

Summarise and check: So, just to confirm, you need the report by Friday, with the March figures. Is that right?
