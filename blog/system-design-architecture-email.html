System Design and Architecture for Email: Which Decisions Are Expensive to Change

Published 7 November 2026 • By Linley Bignoux • Worked example: the Kick Off → Your English abandoned cart email

system-designarchitectureapitransactional-emailbrevodata-designtrade-offsnodejs

The question I started with

Most of what is written about system design is about scale: queues, sharding, load balancing, replication. Most of my systems are not that. They are small: one email, one API call, one client. I work at the point where an email meets an API, so the question I keep coming back to is what system design and architecture mean when the system is one email that depends on an API.

My answer is short. They mean knowing which decisions are expensive to change, making those decisions on purpose, and checking every boundary. The abandoned cart build is the worked example. The email in it is a test version, built for Kick Off → Your English, who have agreed to be named.

Two words, used loosely

People draw the line between architecture and design in different places. A common one is that architecture sets the high-level shape, and design fills in how each part works. That is a fair line. For a small system I find a different one more useful, which is the cost of change:

  • Architecture is the set of decisions that are expensive to change once other people build on them.
  • Design is everything inside my own links that I can change without anyone else noticing.

A decision moves from design to architecture the moment someone else depends on it. People draw the line in different places, and that is fine. What matters is knowing which decisions are the expensive ones before you make them.

The chain, and who owns each link

An abandoned cart email is a chain from the shop's application to the inbox. Every link has an owner and its own way of failing.

The decisions, sorted by cost of change

Here are the decisions in the cart build, sorted by who has to change if I change my mind. The test is simple: if I change it, does anyone besides me have to do work?

One decision crossed the line while I was working. My checks could say a value was present, short enough and safe, but not that a quantity was a number, so a quantity of "one" or a price of "free" would have passed. Adding a format rule was a change to my own checks, which is design. But the rule is also part of what the client's developer builds against, so it became part of the data design I hand over. It is a good example of why the line is not fixed: a rule I write for myself becomes architecture the moment I publish it to someone else.

Trade-offs, and what would make me reconsider

A decision is only half made until you say what would change it.

I choseThe alternativeWhat would make me reconsider
A flat object of six text valuesA nested list of itemsThe client needs a cart with several products in one email
The price formatted by the applicationAn amount and a currency, formatted by the emailThe email has to show totals or convert between currencies
The fallback for a missing name inside the emailThe application handles the missing caseMore optional values appear, so the email's conditions get hard to read
The email found by its ID in BrevoSending the whole HTML with every requestThe client needs to change the wording without a deployment, or the reverse
A send step that is dry by defaultA send step that sendsIt never changes. The cost of a surprise email is too high

The interface is where most things break

In my experience the interfaces between components are where most integration problems live, so the interface is where I spend my care. For the cart email it is six values: the first name, the product name, the product picture's address, the price, the quantity and the cart link, with a rule for each, written down once and checked before anything is sent. Twenty-six deliberate breaks must all be refused, and a generated page, docs/data-design.md, holds the same table for the client's developer. The full detail is in the cart build.

The reason to define it first is the cost of change. A rule written down before anyone builds on it costs nothing. The same rule changed after the client's developer has shipped costs them a deployment, and costs the customer a broken email while the two sides disagree.

That table is also how I trace a problem backwards. If a customer says no email arrived: was a request made, did Brevo accept it, what does Brevo's log say about that message ID, and if it was delivered, what did the customer's email client do with it? Each question moves one link along the chain, and each has an owner. I wrote the symptom-by-symptom version of this in Where Transactional Email Fails.

What sits outside my links

An email system has a lot that sits outside my links, and I would rather name it than pretend it is not there. In the cart system, these belong to the client's application, their domain, or Brevo:

  • A queue between the request and the send, so a spike of abandoned carts does not slow the shop down.
  • Retries with backoff, for when a request to Brevo fails.
  • Bounces and suppression: a hard bounce means stop sending to that address, a soft bounce means try again later.
  • Sender authentication, the SPF, DKIM and DMARC records on the sending domain, without which messages are far more likely to land in spam.
  • Rate limits and the reputation of the sending address.
  • The unsubscribe decision, which depends on the rules where the customers are, and is for the client to make.

I would put one more at the top of that list: make sure a retry cannot send two emails for one cart. A customer who gets the same reminder twice trusts the shop a little less.

The last box: the inbox

Descriptions of the email pipeline often end at the inbox: the provider runs spam filters, categorises, and renders. That is one box in the diagram, and it is where I do most of my work. Dark mode, Gmail clipping an email over about 102 KB, Outlook drawing a button differently, a white-backed logo showing as a white block: all of it lives inside that last box, and none of it shows up in a request, a response status or a log. The system is not finished when Brevo says accepted, and it is not finished when the log says delivered. I wrote about what I find in that box in MJML Is an Abstraction Layer.

Keep it small

I aim for the minimum architecture that gets through the next twelve months. The cart email shows one product because that is what the client needs now. It is also the reason I can say what would change if that stopped being true, and what each option would cost:

  1. Add an optional list of items to the request and keep the old values. Existing callers are untouched, and the email gets harder to read.
  2. Change the request so the single product becomes a list. Every caller must change at once. The most expensive option.
  3. Make a second email with its own ID for carts with several products, and leave the first alone. The application chooses which ID to send. In my experience this is often the cheapest, because nothing the client has already built changes.

I would only know that ordering because I had listed the decisions by cost of change before anyone built on them.

A diagram that cannot go stale

Architecture diagrams go stale, because the real system moves on. I cannot keep a picture in sync with a system by hand, so I generate the part that matters. The table of values the client's developer builds against is written by a script from my settings file and from the email itself, so it cannot drift from what is actually checked. The picture shows the chain. The generated table is the interface.

Takeaway

You do not need a large system to need system design. You need to know the chain, who owns each link, and which decisions other people will build on. Make those on purpose, write down what would change them, define the interface before anyone depends on it, make every boundary refuse what it can and report what it cannot, and name the things that belong to someone else. Everything else, you are free to change quietly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between system design and architecture?

A common line is between the high-level shape of a system and the detail of how each part works. For a small system I find the cost of change more useful. Architecture is the set of decisions that are expensive to change once other people build on them. Design is everything inside my own links that I can change without anyone else noticing.

Do you need large-scale system design to build transactional email?

No. The topics that fill most writing on system design, such as sharding, load balancing and replication, do not apply to one email and one API call. What does apply is knowing the chain and who owns each link, defining the interface first, and making every boundary refuse what it can and report what it cannot.

Who owns queues, retries and bounce handling in an email system?

In the cart system they belong to the client's application, their domain and Brevo. I define the data, write my side of the API and build and test the email. I name the others so that nobody assumes they are covered.

Why is the price sent already formatted?

Because the application knows the currency and the format, and the email should not guess either. Moving the formatting into the email would change both sides, which is why it is a decision with a high cost of change.

How do you change an API the client has already built against?

In my experience the cheapest route is often a second email with its own ID for the new case, leaving the first alone, or adding optional values and keeping the old ones. Changing the meaning of an existing value forces every caller to change at once.

How do you trace an email that never arrived?

I move one link at a time along the chain: was a request made, did Brevo accept it, what does its log say about that message ID, and if it was delivered, what did the customer's email client do with it. Each question has an owner.
