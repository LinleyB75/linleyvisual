Talking About Numbers in English: Figures, Trends and Reports

Published 13 November 2026 • By Linley Bignoux

business-englishnumbersreportspresentationsfinance

The situation

When I worked as a developer, I rebuilt a payment receipt email. Every number on it had to be exactly right and easy to read: amounts, currency, tax, dates and order numbers. One wrong digit, or one figure in the wrong format, and a customer loses trust.

Speaking numbers is harder than writing them. In meetings, people in finance, sales, logistics and engineering present figures out loud all the time, and small errors, like fifteen heard as fifty, cause real problems.

Why it matters

  • Numbers lead to decisions. A misheard figure in a meeting can change an order, a budget or a forecast.
  • Number formats differ between languages. 3,5 in many European languages is 3.5 in English.
  • Clear numbers make you sound confident and precise, which matters most when you're presenting results.

Describing trends

DirectionVerbsExample
Uprise, increase, go up, grow"Online orders rose sharply in March."
Downfall, decrease, drop, decline"Returns fell slightly last quarter."
No changestay the same, remain stable"Costs remained stable."
Highest pointpeak, reach a peak"Demand peaked in December."

How much: sharply, significantly, steadily, slightly. Prepositions: "rose by 10%" is the size of the change; "rose to 10,000" is the new level; "from 40,000 to 45,000" gives both.

Approximating and comparing

  • "About / around / roughly 200 customers"
  • "Just over 45,000 units", "just under 10%", "nearly half"
  • "Twice as many as last year", "compared with the same period last year"

A full example: weak and strong

Weak:

"The sales are more big this quarter, they made a raise of twelve percents, we sold forty-four thousand nine hundred units, before was forty thousand."

Strong:

"Sales rose by 12% in the third quarter, from 40,000 to just under 45,000 units. Most of the increase came from online orders."

The strong version gives the change, the before and after, a rounded figure that's easy to hear, and the reason, in two sentences.

Common mistakes

MistakeInstead ofSay
Wrong preposition"Sales rose of 10%.""Sales rose by 10%."
Plural percent"Fifty percents.""Fifty percent."
Raise or rise"A raise of sales.""A rise in sales." ("A raise" is extra salary.)
Decimal comma"Three comma five.""Three point five."
BillionFrench billion or Portuguese bilião meaning a million millionIn English, a billion is a thousand million (French milliard)

Try it yourself

Take three figures from a recent report at work. Describe each one in a sentence with a verb, "by" or "to", and a reason. Say them out loud, slowly, and stress the teen numbers.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I say decimals in English?

Say point, then each digit separately: 3.75 is three point seven five. English uses a point for decimals and a comma for thousands, so 1,250.5 is one thousand two hundred and fifty point five.

What is the difference between rose by and rose to?

By gives the size of the change and to gives the new level. Sales rose by 10% means they went up 10%. Sales rose to 10,000 means 10,000 is the new figure.

How do I stop people confusing fifteen and fifty?

Stress the end of the teen numbers: fif-TEEN, but FIF-ty. In important conversations, repeat the number another way: fifteen, one five.
