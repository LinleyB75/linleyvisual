Where Transactional Email Fails: Seven Places Between Your Application and the Inbox

Published 3 November 2026 • By Linley Bignoux

transactional-emailtroubleshootingrenderingdark-modetestingchecklist

When a customer says "your email looks wrong", everyone looks at the email. Most of the time, that is the wrong place to look. An email your application sends because of something a customer did, such as a receipt, a password reset or a payment failure notice, passes through seven places before the customer sees it. A fault in any one of them looks the same from the customer's side: something is missing, wrong or unreadable.

This post follows one email through those seven places, the way I trace a problem. I design, develop, program and test the emails I build, so I see each one as a single system, from your application to the inbox, not as a file. For each place it says what the customer notices, why it happens, who fixes it, and how to catch it before anyone is affected. The examples come from emails I have built and tested for Kick Off → Your English, who have agreed to be named, and I say plainly where a check cannot tell you something.

Why it matters

A payment failure email is a good example. If it never arrives, or the button cannot be read, the customer has no easy way to fix the payment, and the client finds out late. Nothing in the email file would show that. The cost of these faults is rarely the email itself. It is the customer who could not act on it, and the support message that follows.

The seven places

Start from what the customer says

You will rarely hear "the data is wrong". You will hear a sentence like these, and each one points to a place to look first and a check to run:

1. The brief and design: nobody asked "what if?"

Many failures are planted before any email exists. A design shows one happy example: a short name, one item, a light background. It does not say what happens with a very long name, no name at all, two items, or a customer who uses dark mode. The developer has to guess, and the guesses come back later as bug reports. When I design the email myself, I answer those questions on the page before I build anything.

In the payment failure email, the amount, the plan and the retry wording were typed into the design. That works for one customer and is wrong for every other. I recorded it as a decision, not an accident: fixed wording for now, with a written list of the values the client may want later.

The answer to a "what if" often fits in one line of the email. A greeting that survives a missing name looks like this:

Hi {% if params.firstName %}{{ params.firstName }}{% else %}there{% endif %},

Who fixes it: whoever designs the email. When a design is supplied, I ask the questions. When I design it, I answer them before I build. How to catch it: a short list of "what if" questions before the build starts: empty, very long, two items, dark mode.

2. The source: a file that says OK and is not

The file behind an email can be broken and still look fine. On the payment failure email, a forgiving build said OK while a strict build refused the file, on an invalid unit, a colour missing its hash and an attribute that does not exist on that part. A closing tag was missing too, and neither build noticed. A style block with one brace left open meant the browser quietly dropped every rule after it. Another client's name sat in the preview text, which is the line shown in the inbox list. A button had no link at all, and in another email a visible support address linked to a different address.

The customer never sees "invalid file". They see a button that does nothing. Faults like these three pass a forgiving build and fail a strict one:

<!-- a strict build refuses all three; a forgiving build lets them through -->
<mj-image src="https://example.com/logo.png" width="120rpx" />   <!-- rpx is not a CSS unit -->
<mj-text color="FFF">Pay now</mj-text>                           <!-- a colour needs its # -->
<mj-text colour="#ffffff">Pay now</mj-text>                      <!-- misspelt: the attribute is color -->

This is what a supplied file looks like when it arrives: the MJML for one of the client's other emails, in an online MJML editor, with a stray closing tag after each line break and file names used as alt text:

A supplied file, as it arrived.

And the email that file builds:

Two more get through even a strict build, so only a report on the built email finds them: a button with no link, and a repeated attribute, where the first one wins. One command builds the email strictly, and refuses the file with the line and the part that is wrong:

mjml email.mjml --config.validationLevel=strict -o dist/email.html

Who fixes it: whoever builds the email, which in my work is me. How to catch it: build in strict mode, read a report on every link, image and line of wording, and compare the result with the original, side by side.

3. The data: where most of the surprises hide

An email is a fixed layout with gaps that your application fills in: the name, the amount, the date, a link. Everything that changes from one customer to the next comes from the data. An empty name becomes "Hi ,". A misspelt field name leaves a blank gap and no error. An amount arrives unformatted. A link text shows one address while the link goes to another. In practice the whole agreement fits on a few lines. This is made-up sample data:

{
  "firstName": "Sam",
  "amount": "19.00 USD",
  "payUrl": "https://example.com/pay?token=SAMPLE"
}

The serious risks are worse. A full card number has no place in an email, and a link that gives access to an account should work once and then expire. On the receipt email, I wrote a check that refuses any value that looks like a full card number. It also reports a field the email never reads, so a misspelt name is caught before a customer sees a blank. To prove it works, I broke the data on purpose six ways, and the check refused five and flagged the sixth. The check itself is short, and plain Node.js:

// check-data.js: refuse bad or unsafe values before anything is sent
const CARD_LIKE = /\b\d{13,19}\b/;

function checkData(data, { required, readByEmail }) {
  const problems = [];
  const notes = [];

  for (const name of required) {
    if (!data[name] || String(data[name]).trim() === "") problems.push(`"${name}" is missing`);
  }
  for (const key of Object.keys(data)) {
    if (!readByEmail.includes(key)) notes.push(`"${key}" is sent but the email never reads it`);
  }
  for (const [key, value] of Object.entries(data)) {
    if (typeof value !== "string") { problems.push(`"${key}" must be text`); continue; }
    if (/[<>]|\{\{|\{%/.test(value)) problems.push(`"${key}" contains markup`);
    if (CARD_LIKE.test(value.replace(/[\s-]/g, ""))) problems.push(`"${key}" looks like a full card number`);
    if (key.endsWith("Url") && !value.startsWith("https://")) problems.push(`"${key}" must start with https://`);
  }
  return { problems, notes };
}

In a finished receipt, the same rule shows up as masked values. Here is a receipt email beside its source:

A receipt email beside its source. The card shows only its last digits, and the address lines are masked.

One assumption causes a lot of trouble: that the email developer sees the real data. They do not. They work with sample data, so the written list of values and the rules are the only agreement between the two sides. If it is vague, each side fills the gap differently.

Who fixes it: the client's developer sends the right data. I define the list of values, which are required, which are optional, and what must never appear, and I program my side of the API call to carry them. How to catch it: a check that runs before anything is sent, and that has itself been tested with deliberately bad data.

4. The platform: set up, but not switched on

The email platform, Brevo in my builds, can fail for reasons that have nothing to do with the email. The email may not be activated. The key may be the wrong kind, because an SMTP key is not an API key. The sender may not be verified. The ID in the request may be wrong. When I rehearsed this on a simulated platform, sending before activation was refused with "not found or not activated", which is exactly what a real account does.

The trap is the word "accepted". A platform accepting a request does not mean the customer received anything. The send step is written so the safe path is the default:

// send.js: the safe path is the default
const body = {
  to: [{ email: process.env.TEST_TO_EMAIL }],
  templateId: Number(process.env.EMAIL_ID),
  params: data,
};
if (mode === "sandbox") body.headers = { "X-Sib-Sandbox": "drop" }; // in the JSON body, not an HTTP header

if (mode === "dry") { console.log(JSON.stringify(body, null, 2)); process.exit(0); } // sends nothing

const res = await fetch("https://api.brevo.com/v3/smtp/email", {
  method: "POST",
  headers: { "api-key": process.env.BREVO_API_KEY, "content-type": "application/json" },
  body: JSON.stringify(body),
  signal: AbortSignal.timeout(10000),
});
console.log(res.status); // 201 means accepted. It does not mean delivered.

Keys stay out of the project, and a guard that runs before every commit enforces it:

# pre-commit: stop a key from ever entering the project
if git diff --cached -U0 | grep -E -q 'xkeysib-[A-Za-z0-9]{32,}|xsmtpsib-[A-Za-z0-9]{32,}'; then
  echo "BLOCKED: something shaped like an API key is staged."; exit 1
fi

Who fixes it: I set the email up and program my side of the API call, and the client's team owns the account, the sender and the key. How to catch it: a dry run that sends nothing, a sandbox call that checks the request, then one real send to your own inbox. Then look in two places: the platform's log and the inbox.

5. Delivery: sent, but not trusted

Mailbox providers check the sending domain's records, which prove an email really came from you. Without them, an email is more likely to land in spam. Those records are set up by whoever controls the domain, so they are not part of the email file at all. This is why "it never arrived" is so often not a build problem. A starting record for a sending domain looks like this, where p=none means watch and report without blocking anything:

_dmarc.example.com   TXT   "v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com"

What the email says matters too. In a two-factor email, I removed a "subscribe to the newsletter" line, because promotional content in a security message blurs what the message is. That is not legal advice, so check with the client if it matters to them.

Who fixes it: whoever owns the domain, usually the client's IT, with me advising and checking. How to catch it: a verified sender, the records in place, and a first real send that reaches the inbox, not the spam folder.

6. The inbox: one email, many screens

Every email app draws the same email a little differently. Gmail and Outlook recolour parts of an email in dark mode on their own, so the email declares both colour schemes and carries separate rules for apps that follow the device and for Outlook.com and the Outlook apps, which mark their dark mode with attributes:

<mj-head>
  <mj-raw>
    <meta name="color-scheme" content="light dark" />
    <meta name="supported-color-schemes" content="light dark" />
  </mj-raw>
  <mj-style>
    :root { color-scheme: light dark; supported-color-schemes: light dark; }

    /* apps that follow the device setting */
    @media (prefers-color-scheme: dark) {
      .bg-page  { background-color: #0f1115 !important; }
      .bg-panel, .bg-panel > table { background-color: #16181d !important; }
      .text-main div { color: #e9ecf1 !important; }
      .text-link a   { color: #9db8ff !important; }
    }

    /* Outlook.com and the Outlook apps mark their dark mode with attributes */
    [data-ogsb] .bg-panel { background-color: #16181d !important; }
    [data-ogsc] .text-main div { color: #e9ecf1 !important; }
  </mj-style>
</mj-head>

Gmail also clips a long email at about 102 KB, which can hide the footer, and link tracking can rewrite a link. A short check after the build warns before either becomes a customer's problem:

// check-build.js: warn before Gmail clips the email
const html = require("fs").readFileSync("dist/email.html", "utf8");
const kb = Buffer.byteLength(html) / 1024;
if (/https?:\/\/(www\.)?example\.com/.test(html)) console.log("A link still points at example.com");
if (kb > 90) console.log(`The HTML is ${kb.toFixed(0)} KB. Gmail clips emails at about 102 KB.`);

7. After launch: emails drift

An email that was right at launch can go wrong later without anyone breaking it on purpose. Someone edits the live email in the platform's editor and damages the layout. A brand change or a new plan is never reflected. A page a link points to moves. A logo hosted on a free image service disappears. The payment failure email's logo is on one, so I flagged that it belongs on the client's own hosting. Every change goes through the same commands, so the live email never drifts from the source:

"scripts": {
  "build":        "mjml email.mjml --config.validationLevel=strict -o dist/email.html",
  "postbuild":    "node scripts/check-build.js",
  "test":         "node scripts/test-checks.js",
  "send:sandbox": "node send.js --sandbox"
}

Who fixes it: the client's team owns changes, and the source file is the true copy. How to catch it: change the source and rebuild, never edit the built email or the live one, and re-test after every change. Keep a build log of what was tested, and where.

Even a small change, such as a new plan name, should go through the same checks. A one-line edit in a live editor skips every check described above.

Treat it as one chain, with one trace

Seven places can have seven owners: the designer, the email developer, the client's developer, whoever runs the platform account, whoever owns the domain, and the team that edits the email later. That is how problems fall between them. Each owner fixes the symptom that reaches them, the same fault returns, and nobody holds the whole picture. When I build the email, the design, the source, the tests and my side of the API are one person's work, so the trace is shorter. The other places still have their own owners, which is why the trace has to cross them.

The end-to-end approach is simple. One person holds the trace from the application to the inbox, and in my work that is me. A problem is traced back through the places, one at a time, and each place is tested on its own before the next one is touched. Fixing the button in the inbox does nothing if the link in the data is wrong. Each place hands the next one a written result:

PlaceWhat it hands to the next
1. Brief and designThe answers to the "what if" questions
2. The sourceA file that builds in strict mode, with every link checked
3. The dataThe list of values and the rules, and a check that enforces them
4. The platformAn activated email, its ID, and a key kept out of the project
5. DeliveryA verified sender and the domain records
6. The inboxTest results from the apps your customers use
7. After launchA build log, and the source as the true copy

When something fails, that list is the map. The log shows which result was missing, and the fix goes there, once.

Before you launch: seven questions

  1. Has the design said what happens with an empty, long or missing value, two items and dark mode?
  2. Does the source build in strict mode, and is every link, image and line of wording the right one?
  3. Is the list of data values written down, with checks that refuse bad or unsafe data?
  4. Is the email activated, the sender verified and the right key in use?
  5. Are the domain records set, and did a real send reach the inbox, not spam?
  6. Did you open it on a phone, in dark mode, and in the apps your customers use?
  7. Who holds the trace from the application to the inbox, who owns changes after launch, and where is the log of what was tested?

What this post does not cover

This is about transactional email built in MJML, sent through Brevo, and tested in the clients mentioned. I have not measured delivery rates or revenue, and I make no claims about either. Other platforms fail in similar places but have their own settings, so check theirs.

Takeaway

When an email looks wrong, find the place first, then fix it, and test that place on its own. Treated as one chain from your application to the inbox, most problems turn out not to be in the email file.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a transactional email?

An email your application sends because of something a customer did, such as a receipt, a password reset or a payment failure notice. It is different from a newsletter, which you send to a list.

Why are email problems often not in the email file?

Because an email passes through several places before the customer sees it: the design, the source, the data your application sends, the email platform, the sending domain and the customer's inbox. A fault in any one of them looks the same to the customer.

How can I tell which stage a problem is in?

Start from what the customer says. An email that never arrives points to the platform or the sending domain. A blank or wrong name points to the data. A button that looks wrong points to the inbox. The symptom table in this post matches each one to a stage.

What does an end-to-end approach to email mean?

It means treating the design, the source, the data, the platform, the sending domain and the inbox as one system, with one trace from the application to the customer. Each place hands the next a written result, and a problem is traced back through the places instead of being fixed where it shows up.

Can an email be tested without sending it to customers?

Partly. Checks can catch bad data and a faulty source, and a sandbox can test a request without sending anything. Only a real send to your own inbox, and a look in real email clients, shows how the email looks and whether it arrives.

Who should fix an email problem?

It depends on the stage. Whoever designs and builds the email fixes the design, the source and how it looks in inboxes, and in my work that is me, including my side of the API. The client's developer fixes the data they send and the call to the platform. Whoever owns the domain fixes the sending records. A good build log says which.
